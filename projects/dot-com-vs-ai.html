Is the AI boom another Dot-Com bubble?

Everyone reaches for the comparison. I wanted to know whether it survives contact with the data — so I dated both eras statistically, picked a bellwether stock for each, and measured which one was structurally more fragile.

  • Methods Chow tests, rolling correlation & beta, GJR-GARCH(1,1)
  • Data NASDAQ Composite, Cisco (CSCO), Nvidia (NVDA)
  • Tools Python · statsmodels · arch

The question

The Dot-Com bubble produced companies with explosive valuations and almost no earnings, and when it broke it cost investors enormously. The AI era rhymes: OpenAI, Anthropic, Oracle, and Nvidia are all spending extraordinary sums on R&D and compute, and the valuations have followed.

The interesting question isn't whether prices are high — they plainly are. It's whether the structure of the market is the same. A bubble isn't just an expensive market; it's a brittle one, where bad news does disproportionate damage. That property can be measured.

How I approached it

Comparing two eras requires deciding when each one started and ended, and that choice drives everything downstream. Rather than pick dates by intuition, I used sensitivity tests to find the months that separate the regimes most cleanly, then confirmed the breaks statistically.

  1. Date the regimes. Use Chow tests to confirm that a candidate date marks a genuine structural break in trend, not just a memorable headline.
  2. Pick a proxy. Choose the company that best represents each boom's infrastructure layer, then validate the choice with rolling correlation and beta against the index.
  3. Measure fragility. Fit a GJR-GARCH(1,1) model to each proxy and compare the asymmetry term, which captures how much harder the market reacts to losses than to gains.

Dating the Dot-Com era

The bubble is visible in price, but it's far more legible in risk. The NASDAQ climbed through the late nineties on low volatility — mostly under 25% annualized — which is what a market looks like when it believes its own story. After the 2000 peak, volatility clusters above 60%. That regime change, not the price peak, is the real signature.

Start: the Netscape IPO, 9 August 1995

I used Netscape's IPO as the entry point into the Dot-Com regime. The Chow test finds a significant time interaction there: both trend acceleration and volatility change measurably around that date, which is what you'd want from a regime boundary rather than an anecdote.

End: 9 October 2002

For the end of the cycle I used the date the NASDAQ hit its all-time low — down roughly 78% from the peak — which followed the Enron and WorldCom fraud scandals. Volatility didn't return to normal until around January 2003, so the low marks the bottom of the panic rather than its resolution.

Cisco as the Dot-Com bellwether

Cisco held the largest market capitalisation at the peak, and it sold the infrastructure everyone else's business plans depended on — the same structural position Nvidia occupies today. That parallel is what makes the comparison worth drawing at all.

Before leaning on it, the proxy needs validating. Rolling correlation against the NASDAQ sits above 0.8 for most of the window, and rolling beta runs consistently above 1 — meaning Cisco didn't merely track the market, it amplified it. A 1% NASDAQ move corresponded to roughly a 2.5% Cisco move.

The AI era's structural break

For the AI era I tested ChatGPT's release on 30 November 2022. The Chow test returns a highly significant break (p < 0.001), and the regression makes the size of it concrete: before the release the NASDAQ was in a secular downtrend averaging −16.7 points per day. The interaction term adds +30.77, flipping the trajectory to +14.0 points per day.

A consumer chatbot launch moving the aggregate index is a strong claim, and it's the result I double-checked hardest. It holds: generative AI registers as a systemic catalyst, not a sector-local story.

Nvidia is not Cisco

This is where the analogy starts to come apart. Nvidia is an industry giant with the same infrastructure-monopoly shape, but it does not lead the market the way Cisco did. Its rolling correlation with the NASDAQ is lower and far less stable, and its volatility is largely detached from the broad economy.

The AI boom, in other words, is more concentrated than the Dot-Com boom was. Cisco was the market; Nvidia is a very large, very volatile object inside a market that is otherwise behaving itself.

Is the panic contained?

So far, yes. Nvidia's annualized volatility repeatedly spikes above 60%, while NASDAQ volatility mostly sits near its 20% norm — a spread of about 20.4 percentage points. Systemic panic would show up as those two lines converging upward. They haven't.

There's a qualitative difference in the fear, too. Dot-Com fear became concrete: Enron and WorldCom were real frauds with real convictions. AI fear is still largely projective — job displacement, political instability, capability worries — none of which has yet produced the balance-sheet event that ends a cycle.

Measuring fragility directly

Everything above is descriptive. To compare fragility as a number, I fitted a GJR-GARCH(1,1) model to each proxy. Ordinary GARCH treats good and bad news symmetrically, which is exactly wrong for bubbles — markets panic downward far more readily than they celebrate upward. GJR adds a term for that asymmetry.

The two terms that matter for this question:

It−1
The panic switch. It equals 1 if yesterday's return was negative and 0 if it was positive.
γ
The leverage effect — the extra volatility added when that switch is on. This is the fragility measure. A larger γ means losses destabilise the stock more than equivalent gains stabilise it.

The result

Both eras show genuine asymmetry — crashes cause more fear than rallies in each. But the magnitudes differ substantially.

The leverage effect was roughly 1.8× larger in the Dot-Com bubble. By this measure the AI boom, for all its valuations, is the structurally sturdier of the two.

One detail in the Cisco fit is worth flagging: its symmetric news term (α) estimates at essentially zero, while γ is large and highly significant. Read literally, Cisco's volatility responded to bad news almost exclusively. Good news did nothing to it. That is about as clean a statistical picture of a bubble's psychology as you're likely to get.

What this doesn't prove

A few limits worth stating plainly, since the conclusion is the kind people like to quote out of context:

  • Nvidia's γ is not significant at the 5% level (p ≈ 0.08). The AI-era asymmetry is weaker evidence than the Dot-Com estimate, which sits at p < 0.001. That cuts in favour of the conclusion — there's less leverage effect to detect — but it's a weaker finding, not an equally strong one.
  • The windows aren't the same length. The Cisco fit uses 1,309 observations against Nvidia's 523, so the two estimates don't carry equal statistical power.
  • The Dot-Com era is complete; the AI era isn't. Cisco's γ is measured across a full boom and bust. Nvidia's covers a boom that hasn't ended. Fragility is most visible in the break, and this one hasn't happened.
  • The trend regressions violate some OLS assumptions — constant variance, autocorrelation, and normality all fail to varying degrees. The Chow tests still fit the historical behaviour hypothesis, but they should be read as evidence for a regime change rather than as precise point estimates.

The honest summary: on the evidence available, the AI boom is less fragile than the Dot-Com bubble was — and the most likely reason is simply that it hasn't been tested yet.
